Why? Why is France stretched across territories thousands of kilometers away from Paris?
The answer to this question is colonialism. To be honest, whenever we talk about the history of Europe, colonialism is often the answer to questions.
From around the 17th century onwards , France became one of the world’s major colonial powers. Just like Britain, Spain, Portugal, and the Netherlands, France had colonies and trading posts around the world. The French Empire controlled enormous areas of territory. There were French colonies in North and South America, the Caribbean, Africa, Asia, the Indian Ocean, and the Pacific.
We’ve talked about some French colonies relatively recently when I discussed the history of Canada and the US Declaration of Independence. Both of those topics were connected to the battle between Britain and France over control of North America.
But after the Second World War, this system of colonialism began to collapse . Not just for France, of course, for all European empires. Across Asia and Africa, people were demanding independence.
European countries had been weakened by the two world wars, and these huge overseas empires were becoming uncontrollable. Keeping them was really difficult.
Britain, for example, gradually granted independence to almost all of its colonies. India became independent in 1947, Nigeria in 1960, Jamaica in 1962, many others in that same period as well.
France went through a similar process. It lost Vietnam after the First Indochina War. Algeria became independent after a violent war in 1962. In fact, during the 1950s and 60s, most of France’s other African colonies also became independent.
But France didn’t believe that every territory or every place had to become an independent country.
In 1946, France made a constitutional change. Several of its colonies, or former colonies, including Guadeloupe, Martinique, French Guiana, and Réunion, became overseas departments.
A department is a administrative division of France. Metropolitan France, so mainland France, is also divided into departments. I guess it’s a bit like a state or a prefecture or a province. In France, they’re called departments.
Legally, these territories, like Guadeloupe in the Caribbean or French Guiana in South America, became parts of France.
The French Republican model, the ideology, the belief system behind France, emphasizes a few key ideas. One of these is the idea of one nation, and connected to that is the idea of one citizenship and equality before the law.
Based on these principles, France made people in these new overseas departments French citizens and made these departments part of France. The people there should have the same rights as French citizens in Paris.
This was quite different from other European empires, like the UK. The UK still has some overseas territories, famously still has overseas territories, but they are not part of the United Kingdom.
They are almost all self-governing , and they are not part of Great Britain or the UK. They are protected by the UK, but they are not part of the UK. Places like Bermuda and the Cayman Islands and Gibraltar have their own local parliaments and they are internally self-governing. They make their own laws. But they are not part of the UK.
The people living there cannot vote in UK elections unless they are living in the UK. But if you’re living in Gibraltar, you can’t vote in the UK election, and there are no members of Parliament for Gibraltar in the UK Parliament.
France did something different to Britain, though. France did something closer to assimilation . Rather than France owning territories, France considered the territories to be French. France expanded its borders. It grew.
