OK then, let’s get right into it and talk about the history of perfume.
As any language learner knows, some words are best left in their original version, not translated.
This is certainly true with French, a language people have long associated with beauty, sophistication , and elegance .
And perhaps nowhere is it more true than when it comes to French words relating to something that France is rightfully very proud of: perfume.
Take, for example, the small town in southern France that is the perfume capital of the world: Grasse. A name that evokes the fields of lavender and jasmine used in the world’s finest fragrances.
But if you translate it into English? Well… Grasse just means “fat.”
And what about the title given to the greatest perfumers—the masters of scent , the artists who create the most luxurious fragrances in the world? In French, they are called “grands nez”—which means “great nose.”
Or, if you were being particularly uncharitable , you might translate it as… “big nose.”
We’ll come to learn more about what it takes to become a “big nose”, or rather a “grand nez”, later on in the episode, but first, we must wind the clocks back and talk about the early days of perfume.
And these early days were early indeed.
The tradition of creating a scent and either covering your body in it or spraying it around a room goes back much further than modern France, further than Rome, and even further than the pyramids of Egypt.
Some of the earliest evidence of creating scents dates back more than 4,000 years to Mesopotamia, in modern-day Iraq.
And, from the very beginning, it had two purposes.
The first was ritualistic ; people burned aromatic resins , woods, and spices as offerings to their gods. The idea was simple: if something smelled heavenly , it was probably appreciated by the heavens .
The other, well, it was for a far more practical reason.
Today we live in a world where most people have showers in their houses, soap is cheap, deodorant is everywhere, and there really is no valid reason to not wash yourself regularly.
But clearly, this is a relatively modern phenomenon .
Go back a few centuries—or even just 100 years—and the world was a much smellier place.
Without modern hygiene, strong body odours were just a part of life.
Sweat , unwashed clothes, the lingering scent of livestock or open sewage in the streets, it was all unavoidable, the world was just smellier.
And so, perfume wasn’t just about smelling nice, it was about covering up reality.
So, how were these early perfumes created?
Well, the process was not too dissimilar to modern perfume making. They would extract the fragrant oils from flowers, resins , and spices, and distil them to leave only the best-smelling scents .
These scents were often used as religious offerings, both being burned in temples, and with priests dousing themselves with them during ceremonies, scents like frankincense and myrrh, which you might remember as being gifts the Three Kings brought to the baby Jesus.
But perfume wasn’t just for the gods; it was also for the living, a way to have pleasant scents that followed you everywhere you went.
And perhaps nowhere was this more true than in Ancient Egypt, where perfume became a serious status symbol .
For the Egyptians, scent was as important as gold or fine clothing.
The wealthy bathed in perfumed oils, covered their wigs in fragrant wax , and carried scented cones on their heads that would melt throughout the day, slowly releasing pleasant aromas .
One theory about why perfume became particularly popular in Egypt is one that you might have guessed.
Egypt is hot!
A burning desert sun, long, sweaty days, and—like everywhere else in the ancient world—no soap, no deodorant, and no running water.
Sure, as a wealthy and status-seeking Egyptian, you might have had people fanning you down and carrying you around in a chariot so you didn’t have to break a sweat , but if you wanted to avoid smelling too much like an overworked donkey, perfume was essential.
And even the dead wore perfume.
The mummification process involved covering bodies in frankincense, myrrh, and aromatic oils, not just for preservation, but so that the dead would smell divine in the afterlife.
And then, of course, there was Cleopatra.
She is said to have been obsessed with perfume.
When she sailed to meet Mark Antony, so the legend goes, she had the sails of her boat soaked in perfume so that long before he saw her, he could smell her.
It’s a dramatic story, perhaps not one based in any historical truth, but it is also a clever reminder of the power of scent: to create an impression, to seduce , and to linger in someone’s mind long after you’ve left the room.
By the way, if you’d like to learn more about the fascinating life of Cleopatra, episode 461 is the one for you.
But, if the Egyptians perfected the art of perfume, the Romans took its use to the extreme.
Romans bathed in scented waters, and doused their clothes, furniture, and even their horses in fragrance .
Some even had perfume fountains at their extravagant banquets , spraying scented mist over their guests.
And, of course, perfume had a very practical function in Rome, because, once again, people smelled bad.
Public baths helped, but let’s not forget that Roman cities had open sewers , unwashed togas , and streets filled with animals.
If you were wealthy, you covered yourself in perfume to stand apart from the sweaty , unwashed masses.
In fact, perfume became so popular in Rome that it started to be imported from all over the world: Arabian myrrh, Indian cinnamon, and North African flowers.
It was a booming industry, and the average Roman senator’s daily perfume consumption would probably even put a member of the Kardashian family to shame .
But with the fall of the Roman Empire, perfume’s prominence faded in much of Europe, at least for a while.
While Europe “forgot” about perfume for several centuries, the Middle East became its new home.
During the Islamic Golden Age, from the 8th to the 13th centuries, Arabic and Persian scientists revolutionised the way perfumes were made.
They perfected distillation , which is a technique that allowed for the extraction of pure, concentrated scents , and is still a method used in modern perfume-making.
And from here, from the Middle East, the knowledge of perfume spread back into Europe, carried along trade routes from the Middle East to Italy, Spain, and to the country that would become the spiritual home of perfume, France.
If you were to ask someone today which country is most associated with perfume, the answer would almost certainly be France.
And it is a well-deserved reputation.
Because while the Romans indulged in excess , and the Middle East perfected the science, it was in France that perfume became an art form.
But why France?
And why Grasse, the small town you heard about at the start of the episode that still calls itself the perfume capital of the world?
Well, strangely enough, it all comes down to leather gloves.
In the 16th century, Grasse was famous for its glove-making industry. But there was one problem: they smelled terrible.
This was real leather , made from the skin of dead cows, and as anyone who has ever spent any time at a tannery will know, the process of making leather smells very bad.
So, local artisans began scenting the gloves with floral extracts to mask the unpleasant odour , to hide the nasty smell.
This small innovation led to a huge transformation.
The demand for scented products grew, and soon, combined with increased taxes on leather , Grasse’s leather industry faded away , replaced by something far more profitable : perfume.
And by the time of King Louis XIV, in the late 17th and early 18th century, perfume was no longer just a luxury ; it was a necessity at the royal court.
The King of France was famously obsessed with fragrance, and the halls of his palace at Versailles were filled with the smells of perfumed waters, scented gloves, and aromatic balls.
At a time when bathing was still rare, perfume became a way of covering up unpleasant realities, not just for individuals, but for entire rooms.
Fountains in Versailles were filled with orange blossom water, and royal furniture was sprayed with lavender and rose extracts .
And it was during this time that the modern perfume industry began to take shape .
Perfumers in Grasse and Paris began refining their distillation techniques, using alcohol instead of oil to create lighter, more wearable fragrances .
These would be packaged up into beautiful little bottles, not too dissimilar to the stylised bottles that perfume comes in today.
And by the 18th and 19th centuries, perfume was no longer just for royalty; it was for anyone who could afford it.
Still, it was not cheap, but it became something that was available not just for a king.
And this brings us to a big question: why is perfume still so expensive?
Even if you don’t buy perfume yourself, you will probably have walked through the duty-free section of an airport or perhaps visited a high-end department store.
And you’ll know that perfume doesn’t come cheap.
A small bottle from a luxury brand can easily set you back €100 or more, and some even sell for thousands.
But, if it’s just smelly water, why is it so expensive?
Well, this isn’t a sponsored message from Chanel or the tourist authority of Grasse, but there are some good reasons.
First, there’s the cost of ingredients.
Some of the world’s most famous perfumes contain materials that are incredibly rare and difficult to obtain.
Take, for example, oud, which is a resin produced by a particular type of tree when it becomes infected with mould. It’s one of the most expensive raw materials in the world, costing more per gram than gold.
And even things that seem more run-of-the-mill , more ordinary, like jasmine and rose, which are used in many classic perfumes. It takes thousands of petals to produce just a few millilitres of essential oil, and harvesting them is a delicate, time-consuming process.
And then there are the synthetic ingredients, which allow perfumers to create scents that don’t exist in nature, like the smell of fresh linen or a salty ocean breeze. These molecules can be incredibly expensive to develop, and some take years of research before they’re ready to be used in a commercial fragrance .
So it’s not so dissimilar to some pharmaceutical drugs, where the raw materials might not be expensive, but all of the research and development that goes into creating them is, so that’s what you’re paying for.
And, lest we forget, there’s the branding . When you buy a bottle of perfume, you’re not just paying for the liquid inside, you’re paying for the name, the bottle design, the advertising, and the luxury experience that comes with it.
Perfume brands spend hundreds of millions on celebrity endorsements , high-profile marketing campaigns, and exclusive product launches. That little bottle on the shelf has been carefully positioned to make you feel like you’re not just buying a fragrance but a piece of a glamorous world.
Of course, not all perfumes are expensive. There are plenty of more affordable options out there, but the reason some fragrances command such high prices is this combination of craftsmanship , rarity , and clever marketing.
And behind every great perfume, there’s a person who created it.
The term for someone who creates a perfume is a “nez”, a nose, and when you get to the top of your game, when you have the most highly-trained nose in the world, this is when you might be given the title of un “grand nez”, a great nose.
Now, becoming a grand nez isn’t something that you or I can “apply for”, and it certainly isn’t something that happens overnight.
It takes years of training, a truly exceptional sense of smell, and an ability to create complex, layered compositions.
It isn’t an official title, and there is no official list of “grand nez”, but it’s thought that there are fewer than 100 worldwide, with some estimates putting it at as little as 30.
In fact, saying “worldwide” is a little deceptive, as most of them are in France, split between Paris and Grasse.
And something that might be surprising, is that despite the fact that most perfume has historically been made for women, the industry of being a “nez”, and especially the “grand nez” at the very top of the profession, has traditionally been a male one.
Now, this is changing; women’s perfume has gone from being around 85% of the market to 60%, and there are more women getting involved in the business of making perfume, but it is a curiosity that it has traditionally been the noses of men that have decided what the noses of women would enjoy.
Now, before we end this perfumed journey, I want to leave you with some interesting facts about the life of a “nez”.
Firstly, although you might imagine one of these “grand nez” to always smell fantastic, and to be covered head to toe with fragrance when they come into the office in the morning, they aren’t.
They have to avoid strong smells, precisely so that there is nothing interfering with their ability to detect the subtle nuances of different smells.
Secondly, and on a related point and bucking the French stereotype , they must avoid garlic, as well as other spicy foods, because they too mean that you can’t differentiate as well between scents.
And further defying certain French stereotypes , most grand nez also steer clear of coffee, cigarettes and alcohol for the same reasons.
Thirdly, apparently, they do most of their hardest work in the morning. This isn’t some productivity hack , but rather that everyone’s sense of smell is most sensitive in the morning. And if your job relies on your sense of smell, well, I guess it does make sense to do the most important work in the morning.
And while the job might seem glamorous , it’s also incredibly challenging.
The competition is fierce , and trends in the perfume industry change rapidly.
One year, everyone wants fresh, citrusy scents; the next, warm and woody fragrances are in demand. A perfumer has to be both an artist and a scientist, constantly experimenting, refining , and adapting.
But if they succeed, their work can last for generations.
Some of the most famous perfumes in history were created decades ago and are still bestsellers today.
Chanel No. 5, for example, has been around for over a hundred years and remains one of the most iconic fragrances in the world.
So the next time you walk past a perfume counter and catch a familiar scent in the air, pause for a moment to think about the journey it took to get there.
From the ancient temples of Mesopotamia to the laboratories of modern perfumers, from the rarest natural ingredients to the synthetic molecules designed to mimic them, perfume is much more than just a pleasant smell.
It’s history, it’s science, it’s art.
And above all, it’s something that connects us, across cultures, across time, through the power of scent .
