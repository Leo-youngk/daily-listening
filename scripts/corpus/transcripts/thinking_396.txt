10 years later, though, I’m still in Japan. I’m now married. I have a daughter. I run a business from Japan. And I can live my entire life in Japanese if I need to.
Looking back, there are things that I’m incredibly proud of in my Japanese learning journey and a few things that I wish I had done differently.
So in today’s episode of Thinking in English, I thought it would be interesting to discuss my past 10 years of language learning experiences: how I went from zero knowledge of Japanese to being able to live in Japanese daily if I need to. Hopefully by listening to this episode, you guys will receive some motivation and some advice about your own language learning journeys.
As I said at the beginning of this episode, I moved to Japan for the first time 10 years ago. I moved to the island of Shikoku to be an Assistant Language Teacher after I graduated from university. In fact, it was my first job, and I had my 21st birthday in Japan.
I had absolutely no Japanese ability when I first moved to Japan. Maybe I had spent a few weeks learning a few Japanese letters. I’d also been given one Japanese class to help me practice my Japanese self-introduction so that I could introduce myself to my new colleagues and other teachers at the schools I would be working at. The way I’m going to structure this episode is to talk about five things that I’m proud of and then five things that I regret.
So the first thing that I am proud of in my learning journey is that I never gave up . I never gave up studying.
I have been studying Japanese continuously, maybe not as consistently as I want to, but I have been studying without giving up for 10 years. This is probably the reason why my Japanese is still at a decent level.
I know people who have lived in Japan for many years, and I know people who are married to Japanese people, but just never committed to language learning. They cannot use Japanese professionally and really, they can’t use it personally either.

Then it became a hobby. I was interested in Japanese. I wanted to learn more. My motivation was enjoyment. This meant that I studied a bit less intensively. When you need to study something, you tend to study harder than if you just like studying.

Sometimes I have studied really intensively over this past 10-year period. Sometimes my progress really slowed down. But I never stopped studying completely.

The second thing I am proud of is going from zero to intermediate level in Japanese in one year.
I started at an absolute beginner level. I did not know any Japanese vocabulary at all. I did not know any Japanese grammar. I did not know Japanese phonics . I had no knowledge of the language at all.
I started to attend local Japanese community classes when I first moved to Japan. Most of my classmates were people working in more manual industries . People working in shipbuilding or ship cleaning or in caregiving.
Initially , I took two group classes a week, and then I added a third class, a private class as well, with one of my Japanese teachers.
I also studied personally myself every single day. I used to finish work, and then on the way home from work, I would stop in at a cafe or a restaurant and study for one hour.
I was fortunate because maybe I had a bit more time, personal time to study than my other classmates. Within six months, I was the top of the class , and then I moved to the next level up in the classes.
I passed the Japanese Language Proficiency Test N4 level, so that’s the second level of the test in my first eight months of studying Japanese. And then one year later, I passed N3, which is intermediate Japanese.
Beginner learners can often underestimate how much progress is possible in a single year. I went from nothing to having conversations in Japanese, and that is something I’m really proud of.
I have friends who moved to Japan the same time as me and never learned anything. I was not going to let that happen to me . I wanted to make progress.
The third thing I am proud of is passing proficiency tests. The main proficiency test for Japanese, the equivalent of the IELTS exam or the TOEFL exam in Japanese, is the JLPT, the Japanese Language Proficiency Test. And I have passed this test now three times.
I passed the N4 level in 2017. I passed the N3 level in 2018. And I passed the N2 level recently, in the past two years. And I’m really proud of these achievements.
The certification itself doesn’t really matter too much to me. I don’t necessarily need it right now in my life. But what matters here is that passing these tests represents years of effort in my life. It is proof that my effort was worthwhile .
The fact I could pass tests is evidence that my Japanese was improving.
I’ve also failed the exams. I failed the N2 exam my first time taking it back in 2021, and I also failed the N1 exam last year. N1 is the highest level of Japanese proficiency test. Hopefully, in the future, I’m going to pass the N1 exam, but it’s not guaranteed.
But I’m still proud of my effort. I’m proud of passing these exams.

And the fifth thing I am proud of in my Japanese learning journey is public speaking. I have been public speaking in Japanese since the first year I started studying.
I have entered speech contests, I have given presentations, I have talked in front of people, and it is really fulfilling .

Let’s move on to some things that I regret or things that I have learned along the way of my studying journey.
The first thing is that I assumed my progress would be linear . Linear means a straight line.
When I first started studying Japanese, those first two years, 2016 to 2018, I improved really quickly. I was going through a chapter of the textbook every week, learning new grammar points and new words every single day. My level was increasing rapidly.
I assumed that if I kept studying as hard as I had been studying, if I kept my motivation and my consistency, my progress would be linear. I would continue making progress. I went from beginner to intermediate. I would go from intermediate to advanced in the same time.
That is not what happened. I assume most of you guys have experienced similar things in the past. I assumed progress would be linear, a straight line. It was not linear.
I had really fast improvement at the beginning, but then I hit a learning plateau. A learning plateau is when your progress stops. Perhaps even language ability deteriorates. You become worse at the language.
There are many reasons why progress would not be linear.
People’s motivation changes. Mine certainly did. Life changes. I moved from living in Japan back to the UK in 2018 and that was certainly a reason for not studying Japanese as much. Now I have much less time , which means I can’t dedicate hours a day to studying Japanese like I used to when I was in my early 20s.
So language learning is not a straight line. Progress is not guaranteed.
The second thing I regret is losing structure.
When I first studied Japanese, I was really structured in my progress. I took three classes a week. I would study for at least one hour a day, usually after work, but sometimes before work as well. I used textbooks consistently.
I knew I had classes. I knew I had study time. I knew I was going to use textbooks. But I have lost that structure now.
I don’t use the textbook correctly. I don’t take classes enough. I don’t study daily. If I have free time now, it’s either spent working or with my family. That’s kind of it. Life is busy.
Once you hit that intermediate level of a language, it’s easy to stop your structure. It’s easy to drift away from what had been helping you work. I had made great progress with having a organized day of studying, and just as I was reaching the peak of my Japanese progress, I moved to the UK. And I stopped studying every day, and I stopped taking Japanese classes for a few months, and I stopped speaking in Japanese regularly.
The third thing I regret from my studying experience is not practicing writing. I think this is something that many of you listening probably can relate to.
I did not practice writing Japanese really at all for the first two years of studying the language.
I did write, I did learn how to write the Japanese characters, some of them, but I just didn’t really practice writing, handwriting especially.
An interesting story is that most of my Japanese writing ability developed after I studied Chinese instead. I started to study Chinese in 2018 when I was doing my master’s degree. I did Japanese as well, but I started to study Chinese, and as part of my Chinese classes at university, I had to write.
Chinese and Japanese partly share an alphabet. The Japanese kanji alphabet is based off the Chinese alphabet, at least the historic version of it.
I assumed, when I first moved to Japan, that I wasn’t going to live here long term , and my focus was purely communication-based. I wanted to be able to listen to people and reply to them. I wanted to be able to have conversations. So I focused extensively on speaking and listening.
I then did a little bit of reading practice as well, and gradually my reading improved, especially when it came to studying for language proficiency tests. But the Japanese Language Proficiency Test does not require handwriting.
So now my Japanese writing ability is far lower than it should be. I have to do paperwork occasionally, and either I need to rely on my wife to help me write in Japanese, or I have to copy the Japanese characters across.
For example, in January when I had to register the birth of my child and I needed to open up my phone, type out the characters so that I could write down the information I needed to write, because I couldn’t remember how to write the specific letters in Japanese.
This is perhaps a Japanese-specific issue in that the letters are difficult to write. You have to remember thousands of different letters. English doesn’t necessarily have that problem.
But also, I never really practiced writing structured sentences in Japanese either.
I think if I had practiced writing more, I would be a better reader as well.
So I really encourage all of you guys to not neglect your writing. I encourage all of you to practice writing in English, because I never practiced writing in Japanese and now I really regret it.

My fifth regret is that I didn’t take advantage of every opportunity I have been offered over the past 10 years. By opportunity, I mean the chance to use Japanese or the opportunity to develop my skills.
