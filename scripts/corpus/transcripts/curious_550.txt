OK then, let’s get right into it and talk about the Egyptian Revolution.
With the benefit of hindsight , most revolutions look obvious.
Whether it is an autocratic leader, an unsympathetic elite, widespread famine, police brutality , systemic corruption, high unemployment, runaway inflation , the lack of political freedom or some combination of any or all of these, it’s easy for historians to look back and say, “the conditions were ripe for revolution”.
Egypt in 2011 would have had a tick in the box for practically all of those things.
High unemployment, inflation, police brutality , lack of civic freedoms, corruption, and of course an autocratic leader in the form of Hosni Mubarak.
But Egypt had had all of these, including Mubarak, for three decades.
The majority of living Egyptians had known nothing else.
On the 6th of October, 1981, Egypt’s third president, Anwar Sadat, was assassinated during a military parade.
His assassins were Islamists who did not agree with Egypt’s increasingly close relationship with Israel and held President Sadat wholly responsible.
With Sadat dead, blown up by grenades , his vice-president, the then 53-year-old Hosni Mubarak, was sworn in eight days later, becoming president of the Middle East’s most populous country.
Mubarak used the threat of Islamic extremism to continue the country’s “state of emergency”, which gave the state heightened powers to arrest, detain , and question if there was even the tiniest inkling of wrongdoing, and in many cases, even if there wasn’t.
Protests were banned, political opponents were jailed, and the secret police kept a watchful eye on anyone who dared to whisper a complaint.
For three decades, Mubarak ruled the country with an iron fist , using the military and security forces to suppress opposition and maintain control over the population.
To the outside world, Mubarak sold himself as a steady hand , a stable and strong leader in a volatile region.
Better a friendly dictator than, well, the unknown.
And for a while, it worked.
The West, especially the United States, propped him up with billions in aid, happy to have a reliable ally who kept the peace with Israel and the oil flowing through the Suez Canal.
For Mubarak and his cronies , his family and his allies, life was good, very good.
One estimate put his wealth at as much as $70 billion.
On the President’s official salary of $70,000 a year, it would have taken him a million years to save that much money, but perhaps he was just a canny investor.
No.
He was egregiously corrupt, and he and his allies siphoned off huge amounts of the country’s wealth and stashed it in Swiss banks and foreign property.
Of course, this was at the expense of the average Egyptian.
Like in any highly-corrupt regime, the loss isn’t just the direct loss of money flowing out of the country to off-shore bank accounts. It’s the fact that business is harder, foreign companies don't want to invest in the country; the corruption drags everything down.
And beneath this international façade of stability, the Egyptian people were suffocating .
In 2011, nearly 40% of the population still lived below the poverty line, scraping by on less than two dollars a day.
Young people, who made up a huge chunk of Egypt’s 100 million-strong population, faced a grim future.
University graduates, full of hope, ambition, and qualifications, found themselves driving taxis or selling fruit on street corners because there simply weren’t enough jobs.
Inflation was eating away at what little money people had.
Bread, a staple of the Egyptian diet, became a luxury for some families.
And if you dared to speak out about it?
Well, the police were more than happy to remind you who was in charge, often with a baton or worse.
The tipping point , or at least what most commentators see as the first domino to fall, came in late 2010, 2,000 kilometres to the West, in Tunisia.
A 26-year-old street vendor called Mohamed Bouazizi had been a regular target for Tunisian police. He was frequently hassled and shaken down for bribes , forced to pay money to the police.
On December 17th, 2010, the police came up to him again.
It’s not clear exactly what happened, and there are varying accounts, but most reports suggest that the police knocked over his fruit cart and took his weighing scales .
With no way to make money and still needing to pay his supplier for the produce that he was planning on selling that day, he went to the local governor’s office to explain what happened and demand the return of his scales.
He had borrowed money to pay for the goods, and now the police had destroyed his only way of earning enough to pay back his debt.
The governor wouldn’t listen.
Bouazizi then went to a petrol station, bought a can of petrol, and returned to the governor’s office.
He stood outside in the street, lifted the petrol can up above his head, emptied it all over himself, shouting, “How do you expect me to live?”
He then lit a match , engulfing his body in flames .
Watchers-on rushed to him and were able to put out the flames . He was rushed to hospital, but he didn’t make it; he died 18 days later.
The story of Mohamed Bouazizi spread like wildfire .
Social media posts, videos, and news reports captured the attention of millions.
In Tunisia, protests erupted almost immediately, and soon the streets were filled with people calling for change.
Within a month, Tunisia’s president, Zine El Abidine Ben Ali, had fled the country.
A dictator who had ruled for 23 years, brought down by the power of a single act of defiance .
And as the news spread, so did the spirit of protest.
In Egypt, the frustration and anger that had been simmering for decades were finally ready to boil over .
Bouzazi was just like millions of other young people all over the Arab world, struggling every day to make ends meet , with seemingly no way out of the situation, and at the mercy of a corrupt police state.
Add to the fire social media - Facebook, Twitter, and YouTube. These became powerful tools for mobilising protestors, bringing people together and sharing information.
Facebook pages sprang up calling for demonstrations, and videos of the Tunisian protests were shared widely.
One page in particular, named “We Are All Khaled Said,” became a focal point for activists. Khaled Said was a young Egyptian man who had been beaten to death by the police, and his story had sparked outrage across the country.
Then came January the 25th, 2011.
It’s National Police Day in Egypt, a day that commemorates the more than 50 Egyptian police officers who were killed by the British army in 1952.
In theory, it’s a day where the Egyptian people remember and honour the police.
But this day in 2011 would be marked for a different reason.
Through social media, people were encouraged to take to the streets not to support the police but to protest against them.
This was very much a grassroots protest: people who organised Facebook pages, people encouraging others on Twitter, it was not led by any one individual or group.
Still, people came out in their droves .
Thousands of people gathered in Cairo, Alexandria, and cities across the country.
Their demands, initially, were more about living conditions and social justice than anything else: the right to live a decent life, free from police harassment .
It was about fairness, the ability for ordinary Egyptians to feed their family and live without the fear of being extorted or murdered by the police.
They chanted for bread, freedom, and justice.
At first, the government’s response was predictable – tear gas , rubber bullets , arrests, the textbook response of an authoritarian regime in the face of public protest.
But unlike previous protests, the demonstrators didn’t disperse . They stood their ground , buoyed by the sense that this time, something was different.
Tahrir Square, right in the heart of Cairo, became the epicentre of the uprising.
Day and night, people occupied the square, camping out despite the threat of violence.
Then, two days later, the country went dark. The largest internet service providers were turned off, as were telephone lines.
Mubarak and his cronies had seen that this movement was being partially facilitated through social media - Facebook, Twitter and YouTube - and clearly thought that simply blocking this, switching it off, would bring about an end to it.
It did not work and only served to light a greater fire in the belly of the Egyptians on the street.
As the days went on, the protests only grew larger, with some outlets reporting that a million people had gathered in Tahrir Square.
The government began to panic.
Mubarak tried to placate the crowds by promising reforms and reshuffling his cabinet , but it wasn’t enough.
The army was deployed, but in a twist , they refused to fire on the protestors.
Soldiers and demonstrators mingled , and some soldiers even showed solidarity with the people.
Mubarak’s position was becoming increasingly untenable .
The United States, which had supported him for decades, was now calling for restraint and even reform.
Finally, on February 11th, 2011, after 18 days of relentless protest, Mubarak stepped down .
The announcement was made by his vice president, and the crowds erupted in jubilation .
The crowds in Tahrir Square danced and cheered, the dictator was gone.
The Egyptians had done it – they had overthrown a man who had ruled their country for 30 years.
But the joy would prove to be short-lived.
Toppling Mubarak was one thing; figuring out what came next was a whole different beast.
For several months in the immediate aftermath, the military stepped in to run things, promising elections.
Meanwhile, political groups that had been crushed under Mubarak, from secular activists to youth movements, trade unions to fledgling political parties, they all scrambled to organise, sensing their opportunity.
But the problem was that Mubarak had spent decades rigging the game , leaving only token opposition.
And the main opposition, or rather, the main apparent opposition, was the Muslim Brotherhood.
This wasn’t exactly a new player on the scene.
It was founded in 1928, and had grown into Egypt’s largest and best-organised Islamist movement, preaching social justice through a large network of charities, schools, and mosques.
For years, Mubarak had banned it outright , jailing its leaders and branding it a threat, yet letting it linger in the background as something of a bogeyman .
Mubarak had always framed it as a simple choice: me, the tough military man you know, or the Islamists.
In other words, better the devil you know than the devil you don’t.
Now, with Mubarak out of the picture, the Muslim Brotherhood saw its moment.
It had a tremendous headstart .
Unlike other political parties, it wasn’t starting from scratch . Its decades underground had built a machine no other group could match.
While secularists and youth rallied in Tahrir Square with passion but little structure, the Brotherhood had members, money, and a plan.
Still, initially, it lacked mass appeal. Egypt might be a Muslim-majority country, but that didn’t mean the majority of Egyptians wanted to be ruled by an Islamic group.
To try to appeal to a broader population, the group softened its more radical edges, talking more about jobs and fairness than Sharia law, and launched a new political party, the Freedom and Justice Party.
This wasn’t the Muslim Brotherhood, it was something new, refreshing, and idealistic.
Not everyone bought it .
To some, especially the urban middle class and Coptic Christians, it smelled like a power grab dressed up as reform; to others, people who had grown weary of corruption and poverty, it offered hope of a new dawn .
Polls from late 2011 showed a mixed picture—about 30% of Egyptians backed them, far ahead of any rival, but trust was shaky, with many fearing an Islamist agenda was hidden behind the smiles and promises of prosperity.
Then, in May of 2012, 14 months after the fall of Mubarak, Egypt held its first democratic presidential election.
The Egyptian people faced a stark choice.
On one side was Ahmed Shafik, a former Air Force officer and Mubarak’s last prime minister, who promised continuity and stability.
A safe pair of hands , albeit not so different from those the people had just overthrown.
On the other was Mohamed Morsi, a softly-spoken former engineering professor and the Brotherhood’s pick to lead its Freedom and Justice Party.
It was close, but Morsi scraped it , with 51.7% of the vote to Shafik’s 48.3%.
Turnout was just over 50%, so Morsi won with the votes of around ¼ of the country’s population.
The Brotherhood had seized its chance.
For the first time, Egypt had a democratically elected president, and Morsi promised a new era of openness and reform.
But he’d inherited a country on its knees . Tourism was practically dead, foreign investors had bolted , and bread lines were sparking riots.
Morsi, like many political leaders, found that governing the country was a lot harder than winning the election.
The Muslim Brotherhood had spent decades as an underground movement, organising charity networks and building grassroots support, but it had precious little experience in the business of actually administering a country, and it showed.
To make matters worse, the military wasn’t exactly thrilled to see an Islamist calling the shots . Yes, Mubarak was gone, but the police and the military were still just as powerful as before, and they were circling like vultures .
Then, Morsi made a fatal miscalculation. He overreached .
In November of 2012, he issued a decree giving himself near-absolute power, claiming it was temporary to protect the revolution from the judiciary, which he believed was still loyal to the old regime.
Then came a new constitution, rushed through by his allies.
Secular and Christian groups walked out, calling it an Islamist takeover, and when it passed in a referendum, barely a third of Egyptians voted.
To many, Morsi wasn’t building a new Egypt, he was turning it into an Islamic state.
Protests erupted once again, but this time, they were directed at the new president.
The sense of unity that had characterised the anti-Mubarak protests was gone, replaced by deep political polarisation.
All this optimism had, within 12 months really, given way to disappointment.
And the army, which had craftily positioned itself as a kind of neutral protector, began to reassert its influence.
By mid-2013, the discontent with Morsi had reached boiling point .
Some polls pegged his approval rating as low as 30%.
Millions took to the streets, demanding his resignation.
But this wouldn’t prove to be a people’s revolution.
The military saw their chance.
On July 1st, they issued an ultimatum : sort this out in 48 hours, or we step in.
Morsi dug his heels , insisting he was the legitimate president, elected by the people, which of course, technically he was.
But legitimacy doesn’t mean much when the country’s against you and the army is itching to act.
On July 3rd, 2013, General Abdel Fattah el-Sisi, the head of the armed forces, went on TV and announced that Morsi was out.
Morsi, it seems, did not have much of a say in the matter.
Tanks rolled into Cairo, President Morsi was arrested, and an interim government was set up.
Sisi, this stern-faced military man with a knack for staying in the shadows, suddenly became the face of Egypt’s next chapter.
To Morsi’s supporters, this was a coup , plain and simple: a betrayal of the democratic dream they’d fought for.
The Muslim Brotherhood and its supporters organised sit-ins demanding Morsi’s return.
For weeks, they held out , but on August 14th, 2013, the military cracked down .
What happened next was called by Human Rights Watch a crime against humanity.
Security forces stormed the camps, firing live rounds into the crowds.
It was one of the bloodiest days in modern Egyptian history, with some reports saying that more than a thousand peaceful protestors were killed by the army.
The Brotherhood was outlawed again, its leaders jailed or executed, but Sisi didn’t stop there.
Tens of thousands—activists, bloggers, even students—were rounded up in a sweeping crackdown that made Mubarak’s repression look mild by comparison.
In 2014, Sisi ran for president, winning in a landslide against a token opponent.
The world watched on with mixed feelings.
The U.S., wary of losing an ally , avoided calling it a coup , while Gulf states like Saudi Arabia cheered , glad to see the Brotherhood crushed.
Sisi had done it, and he has remained Egypt’s President to this very day.
To his fans, he was a saviour, the strongman Egypt needed to stop the chaos.
To his critics, he was Mubarak 2.0: same military playbook, same authoritarian tendencies, just a new face.
Protests were crushed , journalists locked up, and that “state of emergency” Mubarak loved so much?
Sisi brought it back with a vengeance .
The revolution’s dreams of bread, freedom, and social justice faded into the background as stability , however harsh , took centre stage.
So, after Egypt’s rollercoaster ride from Mubarak to Morsi to Sisi, the country is stuck with another military dictator.
Is he any better than the last one? Well, that’s a debate Egyptians are still wrestling with today.
