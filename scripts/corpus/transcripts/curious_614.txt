Now, before we go anywhere, let me paint a picture of where today's episode — and this entire mini-series — will be taking place.
The British Isles, as they are sometimes called, are the group of islands off the north-west coast of Europe.
The largest island is Britain, which is made up of three countries: England in the south and east, Scotland in the north, and Wales to the west. Each of those countries also includes some smaller islands, and there is the added complication of some islands that are something confusing called crown dependencies, but that's too much detail for today.
Just to the west of Britain, across a stretch of water called the Irish Sea, lies an island called Ireland.
It's a smaller island than Britain. It's roughly the size of Portugal. It's green, rainy, and incredibly beautiful.
And today, if you look at a map of Ireland, you will notice something unusual.
The island is divided into two parts. The larger part — twenty-six counties in the south, west, and centre — forms the Republic of Ireland. It is an independent country and a full member of the European Union.
The smaller part — the six counties in the north-east — is called Northern Ireland. It is part of the United Kingdom, alongside England, Scotland, and Wales, and it's governed ultimately from London.
This, confusingly , makes Northern Ireland part of the geographical area of Ireland, the island of Ireland, but separate from the country of Ireland, the Republic of Ireland, to give it its full name.
This division is not some ancient accident of geography.
It is the direct result of decisions made just over a hundred years ago.
And those decisions were themselves the product of a relationship between Ireland and England — and later Ireland and Britain — a relationship that goes back nine hundred years.
This is what this mini-series is about: not the complete history of Ireland, but a journey through the key events that shaped the Ireland we know today.
And one more thing before we begin, let me quickly introduce the main characters — or rather, the main groups — because it can get confusing quickly.
In the twelfth century, when our story begins, Ireland was home to the Gaelic Irish — a Celtic people with their own language, their own laws, and their own system of kingship .
At this point, Ireland was not a unified country. It was a patchwork of kingdoms, clans , and territories. There was the High King sitting loosely at the top, but his authority was limited and often more symbolic than absolute . Irish politics was fluid , competitive, and often violent. Kings rose and fell. Alliances shifted .
To the east, by the twelfth century, England had already gone through one major change of power. In 1066, a group from Normandy in northern France had invaded England under William the Conqueror and taken over the country. These were the Normans.
These Normans were exceptional soldiers. They spoke Norman-French. They were Catholic. And they brought with them a feudal system of land ownership quite different from anything in the Gaelic world, a system in which land was controlled through a hierarchy of lords and tenants .
So when people talk about "the English" arriving in Ireland, what they really mean — at first, at least — is Anglo-Normans.
The ruling class of England was Norman. The soldiers who would cross the Irish Sea were Norman. The language they brought was Norman-French. The name "English" came later.
Right. Now let us begin.
In 1166, a king was on the run .
His name was Diarmait Mac Murchada and he was the King of Leinster.
Leinster is the eastern province of Ireland, the area that includes what is now Dublin.
He had been expelled by the High King of Ireland, with the backing of a rival chieftain named Tigernán Ua Ruairc — a man with a very personal reason to want Diarmait gone.
Years earlier, Diarmait had abducted Tigernán's wife. And back then, like today, this wasn't something that most people took kindly to , and the grudge had never faded .
When he was expelled , Diarmait did what many rulers in trouble do: he went looking for allies .
He crossed to Britain, then to northern France, where he found King Henry II of England.
He asked Henry for permission to recruit soldiers from among his lords.
Now, Henry had no interest in invading Ireland himself. He had territories stretching from Scotland to southern France, and keeping them together was already a full-time occupation.
But he gave Diarmait a letter, essentially a royal permission slip , saying that if any of his English lords wanted to help this exiled Irish king, they were free to do so.
The lord who took up this offer was a man named Richard de Clare, the Earl of Pembroke. History remembers him by his nickname: Strongbow.
And if you have spent much time in British pubs, you might remember this name as a type of not-exactly-delicious cider .
Now, Strongbow was not doing this out of the kindness of his heart . It was a business arrangement. Strongbow would provide soldiers and military skill. In return, he would get Diarmait's daughter Aoife in marriage, and the right to inherit the Kingdom of Leinster when Diarmait died.
Strongbow landed in the south of Ireland in 1170 with around a thousand men. Within months, his men had helped capture the cities of Waterford and Dublin. A year later, Diarmait was dead, and Strongbow was King of Leinster.
Shortly afterwards, Henry II arrived, and not to congratulate him.
Henry was alarmed . A powerful Norman lord establishing a semi-independent kingdom in Ireland, outside his control, was the last thing he had intended . He arrived with a large fleet — reportedly around four hundred ships — and marched into Waterford.
Strongbow submitted to him, and so too did a number of Irish kings.
Now, why did the Irish kings submit so readily?
Well, a few reasons.
Firstly, Henry was the most powerful king in Western Europe. His army was far larger than anything Ireland could field .
But there was something else at work too. Many Irish kings were more worried about Strongbow than about Henry.
A Norman lord controlling Leinster and Dublin was a direct, immediate threat to their own territories. Henry, by contrast, was far away and apparently had no wish to rule Ireland directly.
It seemed like a smarter plan to give a distant king a title on paper than to fight the Normans on their own doorstep .
So they submitted .
In practice, this looked like it meant very little, at first at least.
Henry was not dismantling Irish kingdoms. He was not replacing Irish kings with English governors.
He was simply asking them to acknowledge him as their overlord — to place him at the top of the chain of loyalty, while leaving Irish kings free to rule their own territories as before.
It was, in other words, a relatively light touch .
Henry took pledges of loyalty, brought Strongbow firmly under his authority, and sailed home the following year.
For most of Ireland, life went on exactly as before.
But something important had happened. By 1171, the English — or more precisely, the Anglo-Normans — they had established a foothold in Ireland, and they would never entirely let go .
Now, you will forgive me if I skip forward by a couple of centuries here.
What happened between 1170 and the late 1400s is a long story — and for our purposes, the key thing to understand is this: the English presence in Ireland was real, it existed, but it was pretty limited in practice.
The Normans introduced English law and a feudal system of land ownership quite different from the previous Gaelic model. They built castles, established new centres of power, and reshaped many Irish towns.
But they never controlled the whole island. The area of reliable English rule, which centred on Dublin, was called the Pale.
A pale, P-A-L-E, as a noun, is literally a fenced boundary , an enclosure .
You might know the expression to be " beyond the pale ", and this is where it comes from.
Originally, to be beyond the Pale was to be outside the ordered English world — but the phrase passed into the English language to mean something unacceptable, outside the boundaries of civilised behaviour.
And beyond the Pale, Gaelic Ireland continued much as before, with its own chiefs, its own laws, its own language and its own culture.
And then something happened that you might think was perfectly predictable , but that took the English Crown by surprise.
Many Norman families, who were living side by side with the Gaelic Irish, they gradually absorbed Irish ways . They learned the Irish language. They adopted Irish customs. They intermarried with local families. They became known as the Old English.
The settlers were becoming, it was said, "more Irish than the Irish themselves."
The English Crown spent decades trying to stop this. Laws were passed in the 14th century trying to ban English settlers from speaking Irish, wearing Irish clothes, or from marrying Irish people.
They were largely ignored.
English law in Ireland was, in other words, a paper tiger — a rule that looked powerful on paper but had no real substance when it came to enforcement .
So by the early sixteenth century, meaningful English control had shrunk back to a narrow strip around Dublin. The rest of the island was governed by Gaelic and Anglo-Irish lords who paid little attention to the Crown in London.
All was well and good , but then Anne Boleyn arrived.
Or rather, Henry VIII decided he wanted to marry Anne Boleyn, this led him to break with Rome, and this had knock-on lasting consequences for Ireland.
As a quick reminder, in 1534 Henry VIII officially broke with the Catholic Church in Rome and established the Church of England — a new, Protestant church under his own authority rather than the Pope's.
He wanted Ireland to follow, but it remained stubbornly Catholic. The Irish kept their priests, their Mass, and their loyalty to Rome.
For the English Crown, this wasn't just a religious insult; it was a massive security risk.
In an age of Catholic superpowers like Spain and France, a Catholic Ireland was seen as a potential " backdoor ," a place where England’s enemies could land an army and launch an invasion on London’s doorstep .
So, in 1541, Henry VIII declared himself King of Ireland, an upgrade from the previous title of Lord. This was a clear signal that he intended to rule the entire island, not just manage it from afar .
To secure this " backdoor ," he launched a policy called " surrender and regrant ".
The idea was to bring Irish lords formally into the English legal system by making them English subjects on paper .
Under this policy, they would give up their traditional Gaelic titles , acknowledge Henry as king, and receive their lands back as English-style grants with titles like Earl or Baron.
Some agreed, because it seemed like a practical compromise : recognition from the Crown, while keeping their lands and local power.
Many did not. And over time, those who refused would increasingly be treated not as semi-independent rulers, but as obstacles to be subdued .
And those who did agree often found, in the following generations, that they had stumbled into a trap .
Under Gaelic tradition, land belonged to the clan — it was managed by the lord on behalf of his people, and leadership passed not necessarily to the lord's eldest son, but to whoever the clan chose.
Under English law, land was the lord's personal property, and passed to his eldest son.
So when an Irish lord agreed to " surrender and regrant ," he was unknowingly converting his clan 's shared land into his own private estate under English law. The people who had always farmed that land suddenly had no legal claim to it.
Disputes over who actually owned what would simmer for generations.
Now, Henry VIII died in 1547, leaving Ireland unsettled , only partially subdued , and deeply unconvinced by Protestantism.
His son Edward VI and his daughter Mary tried different approaches — Mary, who was Catholic, actually started the first plantations in the Irish midlands, confiscating land from Irish lords and giving it to English settlers .
But it was Henry's other daughter who would bring the crisis to a head .
Elizabeth I decided that the time for half-measures was over.
Under her reign , England committed large armies to Ireland with a single goal: full military conquest of the entire island, not just the management of the Pale. This transformed the nature of the conflict entirely. We are no longer talking about diplomacy and land grants . We are talking about English soldiers stationed all over the country, and ultimately, war.
The most serious of these conflicts — and the one that came closest to undoing English rule in Ireland entirely — was called the Nine Years' War.
This began in 1593, and was led by a man named Hugh O'Neill, who had been educated in England, who understood English politics from the inside, and who held his title from the English Crown.
An unlikely profile for someone who would become the leader of the last great Gaelic resistance to English rule.
Now, O'Neill was patient , organised, and extremely effective. He trained his men in modern European military tactics rather than traditional Irish methods.
And at the Battle of Yellow Ford in 1598, in the north of Ireland, his forces surrounded and destroyed an entire English army column , killing around two thousand soldiers.
For a moment, it seemed Ireland could actually be lost, or rather, won back, for the Irish.
O’Neill then sought help from Elizabeth’s great enemy, Spain, which was more than happy to assist . In 1601, a Spanish force landed at Kinsale, on the south coast of Ireland. O’Neill marched his army all the way south from Ulster to join them.
But this was the problem: the Spanish had landed far from O’Neill’s power base, and before the two forces could properly unite, the English moved against them. At the Battle of Kinsale, the Irish and Spanish were defeated. O’Neill submitted to the English Crown in 1603.
And then, in 1607, something happened that closed a chapter of Irish history for good .
One autumn morning, Hugh O’Neill and other leading Gaelic lords from Ulster, along with their families and followers, boarded ships and sailed away into self-imposed exile , to the more hospitable Catholic courts of Spain and Rome.
Whether they feared arrest or were seeking military support from abroad, historians still debate .
But they never came back.
This event is called the Flight of the Earls. It marked the end of Gaelic Ireland as a political force. The old aristocracy — the class that had maintained Gaelic culture and resisted English rule for centuries — it was gone.
And the land they left behind would not remain empty for long.
The territories of the fled earls — six counties across the north of Ireland — were declared forfeit to the English Crown.
And in 1610, the so-called Plantation of Ulster began.
Now, Ulster is the northernmost of Ireland's four provinces. It had been the most Gaelic, the most resistant to English rule.
Now it was to be transformed.
The plan was for the land to be divided into parcels and distributed to settlers from England and Scotland. The Gaelic Catholic population who had lived on that land for centuries was pushed to the margins , where farming was harder.
And by law, settlers were forbidden from renting land to native Irish tenants.
Tens of thousands of settlers came, particularly from the Lowlands of Scotland.
Now, Scotland and Ireland have a lot in common. They are geographically close — you can almost see Scotland from the northern tip of Ireland on a clear day — but in the 17th century, Scotland and Ireland, culturally speaking, were very different places.
The Scots were Protestant. They spoke English.
They had no connection to Ireland or its traditions.
The two communities that emerged did not mix.
And of course, they were not supposed to mix.
New towns were built on defensive plans. The settlers and the displaced native Irish lived side by side, but they worshipped in different churches, spoke different languages, and saw both the land and its future in completely different ways: for the settlers , it had been lawfully granted by the Crown; for the native Irish, it had been taken from them.
There is a direct, traceable line from the decision to colonise Ulster in 1610 to the partition of Ireland three hundred years later — the division of the island that you can still see on the map today.
Now, we've already had a few "hate figures", in the form of Henry II and Henry VIII, people whose portraits you won't find on the wall in many Irish pubs .
But they pale in comparison to who comes next, a man whose name is still spoken with bitterness in Ireland today: Oliver Cromwell.
Now, before we get to what he did in Ireland, I need to give you some context on Oliver Cromwell.
In England in the 1640s, there had been a civil war between King Charles I and the Parliament — the body that governed alongside the king.
Cromwell was the military leader of the winning Parliamentary side.
And in 1649, after his victory, he did something that shocked Europe: he put the king on trial and had him executed .
By the way, if you would like to hear more about that, episode 232 tells that story, of when Britain killed its king.
Anyway, after the execution of King Charles I, England became a republic, with Cromwell at its head.
He was a deeply religious Protestant, with an absolute conviction that he was doing God's work.
And he did not conceal his contempt for Irish Catholicism.
He believed the Catholic population of Ireland was a direct threat to England's new republic. And so in August 1649, he arrived in Ireland with an army of around twelve thousand men.
He came to put down a Catholic alliance and to punish those responsible for violence against Protestant settlers eight years earlier.
He was brutally efficient and utterly without mercy .
At Drogheda — a town north of Dublin — the Irish soldiers defending the town refused to surrender. What followed was a massacre . Several thousand people were killed: soldiers, civilians, and Catholic clergy sheltering inside the town.
Cromwell described it in a letter home as God's righteous judgement.
The following month, a similar thing happened at Wexford. Thousands more died.
Cromwell left Ireland in 1650, but the campaign continued for another three years under his officers. The human cost was devastating . Hundreds of thousands of people died — from battle, from the famines that followed the destruction of the countryside, and from disease. A population of perhaps one and a half million in 1641 may have fallen to fewer than a million by 1652.
The land settlement that followed was as brutal as the military campaign . Catholic landowners lost their estates . Those who survived were ordered west of the River Shannon — to Connacht, the western province of Ireland, the most remote and poorest part of the island.
And there's a famous phrase that entered Irish historical memory from this period, "To Hell or to Connacht".
Before 1641, Catholics had owned roughly sixty percent of the land in Ireland. By 1660, they owned an estimated twenty percent.
And the Cromwellian settlement created the social structure that would define Ireland for the next two centuries.
A Protestant landowning class sat at the top.
A Catholic majority of tenants and workers lived beneath them, on land that had once been their own, or their parents', or their grandparents'.
Everyone knew it. The awareness of that dispossession ran like a fault line through everything that followed.
Almost five centuries earlier, the exiled King Diarmait had invited the English over to Ireland.
It would prove to be a decision that would forever change the country, and one that the people of Ireland would spend the next 800 years trying to undo.
